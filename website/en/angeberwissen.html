Fun facts with Ludwig

Baaah! Things not everyone knows about structure charts.

I am Ludwig, the goat of VisuStruct. I got my name from the Ludwig-Geißler-Schule, a school in Hanau, Germany. Here I tell you who invented the structure chart, why nobody wanted to print it at first and what the two inventors built afterwards. Every fact comes with a source, so you can really show off in class.

The story

How 15 minutes of doodling became a standard

  1. 1972: The idea during a talk

    Ben Shneiderman was a PhD student at Stony Brook University near New York. During a talk about structured programming he thought: if we should do without GOTO, why not also do without the arrows in a flowchart? After about 15 minutes of sketching, sequence, branch and loop were on paper.

    Source: Shneiderman, A short history of structured flowcharts

  2. Two heads are better than one

    Back at the university, his fellow PhD student Isaac “Ike” Nassi helped him. Nassi knew structured programming especially well. That is why the diagram is named after both of them: the Nassi-Shneiderman diagram.

    Source: Shneiderman, A short history of structured flowcharts

  3. Rejected first, famous later

    The journal Communications of the ACM quickly rejected the article. According to Nassi, the reviewer even said they should collect all copies and burn them. So it appeared in August 1973 in ACM SIGPLAN Notices, a newsletter without reviewers. Today the article has been cited thousands of times.

    Sources: Nassi & Shneiderman, Flowchart techniques for structured programming, SIGPLAN Notices 8(8), 1973, pp. 12–26 · Oral History of Ike Nassi, Computer History Museum, 2016

  4. An official standard in Germany

    In Germany the diagrams are called “Struktogramme”. Since November 1985 they have been defined in the standard DIN 66261. That is why they still appear in German textbooks and computer science classes today.

    Source: Wikipedia: Nassi–Shneiderman diagram

  5. Watch out, spelling trap

    It is Shneiderman, without a c. Shneiderman himself points out that his name is misspelled in many sources. If you know that, you are allowed to show off a little.

    Source: Shneiderman, A short history of structured flowcharts

  6. Used in industry too

    According to Shneiderman, the diagrams were used at IBM, Siemens, Bell Labs, AT&T, GE and the US Navy, among others. Microsoft Visio and many other programs come with templates for them.

    Source: Shneiderman, A short history of structured flowcharts

Why it is so clever

What structure charts do better than flowcharts

Ludwig sums up why the idea from 1972 is still used in class today.

No spaghetti code

There are no arrows, so there are no wild jumps either. Every block has exactly one entry and one exit. That is exactly what structured programming asks for.

Nesting at a glance

You see a loop inside a branch right away, because the blocks sit inside each other. Where a block ends, you can see from its border.

Independent of the language

A structure chart can be turned into Java, Python or JavaScript. You think about the steps first and about the syntax later.

The inventors

What became of the two

Ben Shneiderman

born 1947 in New York · computer scientist

  • PhD from Stony Brook University (1973).
  • Taught at the State University of New York and Indiana University, then at the University of Maryland. There he founded the Human-Computer Interaction Lab in 1983 and led it until 2000. He officially retired in 2017 but still does research and writes.
  • A pioneer of human-computer interaction: in 1982 he coined the term “direct manipulation”, which means grabbing and moving things on the screen directly, like the elements in VisuStruct.
  • Invented the treemap (1991). In 1985 his team studied which colour works best for links and ended up with light blue. In 1988 it came up with the idea for touchscreens to act only when you lift your finger. His research also led to the company Spotfire.
  • His “eight golden rules” for user interfaces are still taught today. Since 2010 he has been a member of the National Academy of Engineering.

Sources: Shneiderman, About · Wikipedia: Ben Shneiderman · Mozilla: Why are hyperlinks blue?

Isaac “Ike” Nassi

born 1949 · computer scientist and entrepreneur

  • Studied computer science and earned his PhD at Stony Brook University.
  • Helped design the programming language Ada and received an award from the US Department of Defense for it in 1983.
  • Worked at Apple, finally as Senior Vice President of Software. His lab developed the Dylan language for the Apple Newton.
  • Later Chief Scientist at SAP and founder of the company TidalScale.
  • Taught computer science at Boston University early on. Since 2011 he has been an adjunct professor at the University of California, Santa Cruz. He is a trustee of the Computer History Museum and holds more than 40 patents.

Sources: nassi.com · Wikipedia: Ike Nassi

From their lives

Stories Ludwig especially likes

  1. A date in the computer center

    Nassi met his wife in the computer center. Back then, a Saturday night date meant: she punched his punch cards. According to Nassi, she still tells that story today.

    Source: Oral History of Ike Nassi, Computer History Museum, 2016

  2. A university in the mud

    When Nassi came to Stony Brook in 1966, the campus was still a building site. Nassi says it was all mud. Still, the university was seen as the “Berkeley of the East”. He started with biology and switched to mathematics after the first year.

    Source: Oral History of Ike Nassi, Computer History Museum, 2016

  3. A favor between friends

    So that Shneiderman could spend a full year at the university, he gave up his teaching job at a college. Nassi took over the numerical mathematics course Shneiderman had taught there.

    Source: Oral History of Ike Nassi, Computer History Museum, 2016

  4. Don’t get “ike’d”

    Just before Nassi’s PhD defense, someone else proved exactly his theorem, one week earlier. After that, people in the department joked: watch out that you don’t get “ike’d”. Nassi passed anyway, but he had to do extra work.

    Source: Oral History of Ike Nassi, Computer History Museum, 2016

  5. Turning a rejection into a speech

    Years later, Shneiderman was invited to Stony Brook to give a ceremonial speech. According to Nassi, his topic was the crushing rejection from back then. The message: just keep going. In 2015 Stony Brook even gave him an honorary doctorate.

    Sources: Oral History of Ike Nassi · Shneiderman, About

  6. A family of storytellers

    Shneiderman’s father S. L. Shneiderman was a journalist and reported from the Spanish Civil War from 1936 to 1938. His uncle David Seymour, known as “Chim”, was a photographer and co-founder of the famous photo agency Magnum. Ben takes photos too: he has taken thousands of pictures at computer science conferences and portraits of computing pioneers.

    Sources: Wikipedia: S. L. Shneiderman · Shneiderman, About
